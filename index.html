A $40,000 lifestyle is a $51,282 withdrawal

The 4 percent rule is always quoted on net spending: a $1,000,000 portfolio at 4 percent pays $40,000, and the budget is built on that number. The withdrawal clears the tax line before it clears the budget. At a 22 percent bracket, $40,000 of after-tax spending requires $51,282 of gross withdrawal, $40,000 divided by 0.78, which is an effective 5.13 percent of the same portfolio. Every $100 of lifestyle pulls $128.21 out of the account. The ladder reprices at every bracket step: a 10 percent bracket turns the rule into 4.44 percent, 12 percent into 4.55, 22 into 5.13, and 24 into 5.26.

The compounding ledger runs year by year on $1,000,000 growing at 5 percent, one retiree withdrawing $40,000 net and one withdrawing the grossed $51,282. At year 10 the balances read $1,100,623 against $951,623, a gap of $149,000. Year 20: $391,704 apart. Year 30: $1,531,511 against $744,466, a gap of $787,045. The extra withdrawals only sum to $338,462 over those 30 years; the statement shows 2.3 times that, because each extra $11,282 also loses the growth it would have earned in every later year. One path ends at 1.53 times the starting stack, the other at 0.74.

The fix is arithmetic, not optimism: for 4 percent of a portfolio to net $40,000 at a 22 percent bracket, the portfolio is $1,282,051, which is $282,051 more than the quote assumes, about 28 percent extra stack. The 4 percent number is not wrong, it is pre-tax, and on a taxable withdrawal the tax line is the largest single expense in the plan, larger than any fund fee it gets compared against.
